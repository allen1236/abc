Reset Minimization in Sequential Circuits via Bounded
Initialization Sequences
Anonymous Author(s)

Abstract
Resets in sequential circuits incur significant area and routing over-
head. Existing reset-removal methods rely on observability don’t-
cares (ODCs) but do not synthesize initialization sequences, while
initialization techniques typically target arbitrary known values
rather than user-specified ones. We propose a scalable framework
for reset minimization that realizes user-specified initial conditions
up to functional equivalence. The target is expressed as a partial
register assignment, reflecting practical scenarios and expanding
the optimization space. Our method combines timeframe expansion
with Maximum Satisfiability (MaxSAT) to derive reset specifications
and initialization sequences under a three-valued approximation,
followed by exact SAT-based refinement to reduce pessimism. Ex-
periments on ISCAS’89 and ITC’99 benchmarks show that, under
a 10-minute runtime budget per design for the iterative MaxSAT
stage, the proposed approach eliminates 43.75% of required resets
on aggregate.

1 Introduction
Initializing a sequential design into a desired operating condition is
a fundamental requirement at startup, after power-up, and during
recovery from exceptional events. In this work, we use reset to
refer to a dedicated reset mechanism that directly forces registers
to prescribed values when a reset signal is asserted, rather than
scan-based or sequence-based initialization. Although such reset
provides a convenient and predictable way to establish an initial
condition, it is not free: resettable registers typically require larger
cells, additional routing resources, and may exacerbate congestion,
power, and timing issues [6, 19]. As modern designs continue to
scale in size and complexity, reducing unnecessary reset while
preserving correct initialization becomes an increasingly important
optimization objective.

A line of prior work addresses reset reduction through redundant-
reset detection and related analyses of initialization necessity. In
particular, sequential-analysis-based methods identify resets whose
values cannot affect the observable behavior and can therefore
be safely removed [19]. Related work has also shown that, under

bounded startup behavior, some registers need not be initialized,
enabling reductions in initialized registers and associated routing
overhead [6]. Such techniques are highly relevant from the stand-
point of area, power, and routability. However, they do not exploit
initialization sequences to realize a user-required startup condition.
A different line of work studies circuit initialization through
primary-input sequences [13, 15, 17, 18]. These works aim either to
initialize as many registers as possible or to initialize all registers
with the minimum number of resettable registers. However, they
focus on driving registers into arbitrary known values rather than
satisfying an explicitly specified startup requirement.

To bridge this gap, we study reset minimization with initializa-
tion sequences under an explicit user-specified startup requirement.
We represent this requirement as a reset vector, i.e., an assignment
over all registers in which each entry is 0, 1, or unspecified. Impor-
tantly, the reset vector is not treated as a literal bitwise state that
must be matched exactly; rather, it specifies the startup behavior to
be realized. This reflects the practical observation that only part of
the register state may matter at startup, and requiring all registers
to be initialized can introduce unnecessary reset with no actual
benefit [6].

Under this formulation, the initialization sequence is used to
compensate for removed reset while still realizing the initial reset
vector. Our objective is therefore not merely to drive registers into
arbitrary known values, as in prior circuit initialization works,
but to realize the required startup behavior with as little reset as
possible. This leads to a different correctness notion: safe realization
of the initial reset vector.

At a broader level, related notions also appear in automata theory
and test generation, such as synchronizing sequences [10, 16], hom-
ing sequences [12, 14], and ATPG-style state-steering formulations
[5, 9]. These approaches are conceptually adjacent to our problem
in that they also use primary-input sequences to reduce uncertainty
in sequential behavior, either by steering the system toward certain
states or by resolving ambiguity relevant to testing. However, their
objectives are different: synchronizing- and homing-sequence for-
mulations are typically concerned with the existence or length of
such sequences, whereas ATPG-style formulations focus on testing
goals such as fault activation, propagation, or coverage.

To solve the proposed formulation, we begin from the initial reset
vector, and derive a more tractable sufficient condition for safe re-
alization. This condition enables an optimization framework based
on timeframe expansion. We then encode the resulting problem
using three-valued logic and formulate a MaxSAT instance whose
solution simultaneously determines a final reset vector and an
initialization sequence. Because the 3-valued abstraction is conser-
vative, we further incorporate an exact SAT-based refinement step
to recover solutions that may be excluded by the overapproxima-
tion. In this way, the proposed optimization flow remains scalable
while preserving correctness under the original formulation.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

• We formulate a new initialization problem that combines
reset minimization with initialization sequences under an
explicitly specified startup requirement.

• We introduce safe realization for initial reset vectors with
unspecified entries, thereby distinguishing our formulation
from prior circuit-initialization methods that target arbitrary
known values.

• We derive a scalable optimization framework based on a
sufficient structural condition, timeframe expansion, and
MaxSAT encoding.

• We incorporate an exact SAT-based refinement procedure to
mitigate the conservatism of the stronger surrogate condi-
tion.

• Experimental results on the ISCAS’89 and ITC’99 bench-
marks demonstrate that the proposed method can substan-
tially reduce reset while preserving the required startup
behavior.

2 Preliminaries
2.1 Boolean Network
A combinational Boolean netlist can be modeled as a directed acyclic
graph (DAG) where nodes represent logic gates and edges repre-
sent interconnections. The source and sink nodes are primary in-
puts (PIs) and primary outputs (POs), respectively. For a sequential
netlist, the presence of state-holding elements (flip-flops) introduces
cyclic dependencies. To analyze the sequential behavior combina-
tionally, we model the circuit by breaking the feedback loops at the
registers. We decompose each register into a present-state node
𝑟 ∈ 𝑅 (acting as a pseudo-PI) and a next-state node 𝑟 ′ ∈ 𝑅′ (acting
as a pseudo-PO). Consequently, the sequential circuit is abstracted
as a purely combinational DAG 𝐺 = (𝑉 , 𝐸), where the node set
consists of PIs, POs, 𝑅, 𝑅′, and logic gates. In this work, the union
of PIs and 𝑅 is referred to as combinational inputs (CIs), and the
union of POs and 𝑅′ is referred to as combinational outputs (COs)
While our proposed methodology is applicable to general gate-
level netlists, we formulate and implement our approach using the
and-inverter graph (AIG) for structural and computational efficiency.
An AIG restricts the internal logic nodes to exclusively two-input
AND gates. Inversions are not physical nodes but are represented
as boolean attributes on the directed edges 𝐸.

2.2 3-Valued Logic and AIG Evaluation
To analyze the logic evaluation unknown starting states and pri-
mary inputs, we employ 3-valued logic with T = {0, 1, 𝑋 }, where 𝑋
denotes an unknown or undetermined value. In the sequel, we use
the hat symbol “ˆ” to denote any bit vector, operator, or function
in the 3-valued domain T.

Any standard Boolean function 𝑓 : B𝑛 → B can be lifted to its
ternary extension ˆ𝑓 : T𝑛 → T. In our structural AIG representation,
the ternary evaluation is performed node-by-node according to
Kleene’s semantics:

• Inverter: ˆ¬(0) = 1, ˆ¬(1) = 0, and ˆ¬(𝑋 ) = 𝑋 .

• AND gate: For 𝑐 = ˆ∧(𝑎, 𝑏), the output 𝑐 = 0 if at least one
input is 0 (the controlling value). The output 𝑐 = 1 if and
only if both inputs are 1. In all other cases, 𝑐 = 𝑋 .

While 3-valued logic enables efficient linear-time evaluation of a
network with unknown input or register assignment, it inherently
suffers from X-pessimism due to the loss of spatial correlation be-
tween internal signals. Consequently, any signal that evaluates to
a 0 or 1 in the ternary domain is guaranteed to hold that constant
value in the Boolean domain (a safe over-approximation). However,
a signal evaluated to 𝑋 could still be a Boolean constant in exact
logical analysis.

2.3 Satisfiability and Maximum Satisfiability
Given a Boolean formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF), the
satisfiability (SAT) problem [7] asks whether there exists an assign-
ment to its variables that satisfies all clauses. SAT is widely used
in logic synthesis, verification, and test generation as a general
framework for exact Boolean reasoning.

The maximum satisfiability (MaxSAT) problem [1] extends SAT
by optimizing the number of satisfied clauses. In particular, a MaxSAT
instance consists of two sets of clauses: a set of hard clauses, all of
which must be satisfied, and a set of soft clauses, of which as many
as possible should be satisfied simultaneously. Therefore, MaxSAT
provides a natural way to express combinational or sequential
constraints exactly through the hard clauses, while encoding an
optimization objective through the soft clauses.

4 Proposed Method
The overall flow is shown in Fig. 2. First, we derive a target-induced
constant cut as a sufficient condition for safe realization (Section 4.1).
Second, we unroll the circuit over a given timeframe length and
solve a MaxSAT formulation to obtain a final reset vector and
initialization sequence (Section 4.2). We repeat this process with
different timeframe lengths to search for a best baseline solution
(Section 4.3). Finally, we apply an exact SAT-based refinement to
recover Boolean relations missed by the 3-valued approximation
(Section 4.4).

4.3 Iterative MaxSAT Solving
The fixed-timeframe Partial MaxSAT formulation in Section 4.2
returns an optimal solution only for the chosen number of time-
frame length 𝑘. In practice, however, the number of resets in the
final reset vector depends strongly on the initialization-sequence
length. A small 𝑘 may lead to overly restrictive solutions, whereas
a large 𝑘 significantly increases the size of the unrolled 3-valued
instance and may become computationally prohibitive. Therefore,
instead of committing to a single 𝑘 in advance, we solve a sequence
of fixed-timeframe subproblems and retain the best solution found
within the time budget as a heuristic.

frame lengths. For each 𝑘 ∈ K, we construct the MaxSAT instance
with hard clauses Φ(𝑘 )

4.4 SAT-based Refinement
The MaxSAT solution ( ˆ𝜌, Π) obtained from Section 4.3 is conser-
vative. The baseline enforces the constant-cut condition in the
3-valued domain; therefore, some concrete entries in the final reset
vector ˆ𝜌 may not be necessary under exact Boolean reasoning. The
goal of refinement is to release as many entries of ˆ𝜌 as possible
from {0, 1} to 𝑋 , while preserving safe realization.

Directly checking the original safe-realization condition would
require the miter in Fig. 1, which requires an existential search of a
witness state compatible with the initial reset vector. Instead, we
use a stronger miter based on the witness construction described
in the proof of Theorem 1. For a reached state at timeframe 𝑘, we
construct a witness state 𝜎 ∗ as follows:

𝜎 ∗|𝑅reset = ˆ𝜎 |𝑅reset,

,

where 𝜎𝑘 is the reached state of the unrolled initialized circuit. That
is, the reset registers of the witness are fixed by the initial reset

vector, while the free registers are bound to the corresponding
reached registers at timeframe 𝑘.

The resulting Boolean miter, illustrated in Fig. 4, contains two
copies of the circuit. The actual copy is unrolled for 𝑘 timeframes,
initialized by the final reset vector ˆ𝜌, and driven by the initialization
sequence Π. The reference copy is a single-frame circuit initialized
by the witness state 𝜎 ∗. At the comparison timeframe, both circuits
share the same PI assignment, and all COs are compared. This
binding removes the existential search over compatible states in the
refinement check. Consequently, the original ∀∃∀ safe-realization
condition is replaced by a stronger witness-bound ∀∀ condition:

cause Eq. (11) contains only universal quantifiers, we check it by
constructing the SAT encoding from the negation of the miter. The
miter output is the disjunction of all CO mismatches between the
actual circuit and the witness-bound reference circuit. Thus, a satis-
fying assignment corresponds to a counterexample: a completion of
the currently free reset entries and a comparison PI assignment un-
der which some CO differs. If this SAT encoding is UNSAT, then no
such counterexample exists, and the witness-bound ∀∀ equivalence
condition holds.

Eq. (11) is stronger than the original safe-realization definition
because the existential witness is fixed by the binding construction
instead of being chosen freely. Therefore, every solution that passes
the check is sound with respect to safe realization, but some valid
solutions may be rejected. Hence, the refinement is sound but not
complete.

5 Experimental Results
The proposed method was implemented in C/C++ on top of the
open-source logic synthesis framework ABC [3]. All experiments
were conducted on a workstation with Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS, 48 CPUs,
and 256 GB memory. EvalMaxSAT [2] was adopted for MaxSAT
solving.

We evaluate the proposed method on the sequential ISCAS’89 [4]
and ITC’99 [8] benchmark suites. All benchmarks were processed
by ABC using commands strash;&dc2 [11]. The resulting circuit
statistics were reported in Table 1, including AIG node and register
(latch) counts.

Given a circuit and a specified fraction of reset registers, we
randomly generated 10 initial reset vectors for evaluation. Unless
otherwise stated, all reported results are averaged over 10 random
initial reset vectors.

5.1 Results Under Full Initial Reset
We evaluated the proposed flow on circuits with fully specified (no
X-value) initial reset vectors, with a 600-second runtime budget for
iterative MaxSAT solving per circuit under an initial reset vector.
Afterward, SAT-based refinement (Section 4.4) was applied to the
best solution found. For circuit s38584, only a single UNSAT-core-
based refinement was performed due to the high cost of full iteration.
The reported runtime includes both stages.

Table 1 presents per-benchmark results, including the numbers
of primary inputs/outputs, AIG nodes, registers, the baseline reset
ratio (with initialization sequence length 𝑘 = 0), the best ratio from
iterative MaxSAT before refinement, the final ratio after refine-
ment, the selected timeframe length, total runtime, and refinement

runtime. The last rows report both the arithmetic mean and the
aggregate reset ratio over all benchmarks.

As seen from Table 1, the proposed iterative MaxSAT flow sub-
stantially reduces the reset requirement compared with the 𝑘 = 0
baseline. On the aggregate statistics, the reset ratio decreases from
90.83% at 𝑘 = 0 to 56.52% before refinement, and further to 56.25% af-
ter refinement. The results demonstrate that even under a practical
fixed runtime budget, short initialization sequences can signifi-
cantly reduce the required reset registers.

A closer examination of the per-benchmark results reveals two
trends. First, at the trivial baseline (𝑘 = 0), many benchmarks
exhibit reset ratios near 100%, indicating that most registers must
be explicitly reset. After iterative optimization, these ratios drop
significantly, with some benchmarks reaching zero-reset solutions
even before refinement. Second, although the refinement stage
typically yields smaller improvements than the MaxSAT search, it
remains beneficial for harder cases and is particularly effective when
the initial reset vector is relaxed from fully specified to partially
specified, as will be discussed in Section 5.3.

Although the initial reset vectors in this experiment are fully
specified, the final initialized states do not always match them bit
by bit. This suggests that exact per-register matching is stronger
than necessary and motivates the safe-realization formulation. The
next subsection isolates this effect through an ablation study, while
Section 5.3 further examines how additional unspecified entries in
the initial reset vector affect reset reduction.

5.2 Direct Register Matching vs. Safe

Realization

A key difference from prior reset minimization formulations is that
we do not require bitwise matching of the specified reset registers;
instead, correctness is defined via safe realization (Definition 1). To
isolate the impact of this modeling choice, we conduct an ablation
study comparing two target formulations on the same benchmarks
and initial reset conditions with a fixed timeframe length 𝑘 = 4.
Refinement is disabled to ensure the results reflect only the effect
of the induced constant cut. In the first formulation, Direct Match-
ing, the final state at timeframe 𝑘 is required to match all specified
target registers exactly. In the second, we use the proposed safe
realization induced by the initial reset vector. Thus, the only differ-
ence is whether correctness is enforced via per-register equality or
functional safe realization.

5.4 Effects of Initialization Sequence Length
We examine how the initialization-sequence length affects reset
reduction and runtime. Circuits s9234 and b14 are selected for
demonstration. For each benchmark, we solve the fixed-timeframe
MaxSAT formulation over a range of timeframe lengths and report
the pre-refinement result for both fully specified and 50%-specified
initial reset vectors.

Figure 5 shows that increasing 𝑘 generally improves reset re-
duction, but the improvement eventually saturates. This behavior
supports the iterative search strategy in Section 4.3: small 𝑘 values
are often insufficient, while very large 𝑘 values provide limited
additional reset reduction but substantially increase the size of the
unrolled MaxSAT instance.

The runtime curves are more instance-dependent. For b14, the
50%-specified and fully specified settings have similar fixed-𝑘 run-
times, whereas for s9234, the 50%-specified setting can be slightly
slower at the same 𝑘. Thus, relaxing the initial reset vector does not
uniformly simplify each fixed-𝑘 MaxSAT instance. Nevertheless,
around the point where the reset ratio reaches zero or becomes
close to its saturated value, runtime often exhibits a small local

6 Conclusion
This paper studied reset minimization for sequential circuits under a
user-specified initial reset vector. Unlike prior circuit-initialization
methods that mainly drive registers into arbitrary known values,
our formulation aims to realize a specified startup requirement
with fewer reset registers. Correctness is defined by safe realization,
which allows the initialized state to differ from the initial reset
vector as long as it is functionally equivalent at the COs to some
compatible state.

To make the problem scalable, we proposed a flow based on a
target-induced constant cut, 𝑘-timeframe 3-valued MaxSAT encod-
ing, iterative search over initialization lengths, and exact SAT-based
refinement. The constant cut provides a sufficient condition that
avoids the original ∀∃∀ safe-realization check, while the refine-
ment step recovers part of the conservatism introduced by 3-valued
reasoning.

Experimental results on ISCAS’89 and ITC’99 benchmarks show
that the proposed method substantially reduces reset cost under
practical time budgets. Under fully specified initial reset vectors, the
aggregate reset ratio decreases from 90.83% at 𝑘 = 0 to 56.52% after
iterative MaxSAT search, and further to 56.25% after refinement.
Additional experiments show that safe realization is less restrictive
than exact register matching and that partial initial reset vectors
further improve reset reduction and runtime by allowing high-
quality solutions at shorter initialization lengths.

Future work includes deriving less conservative approximations,
strengthening the current refinement step, and studying integration
with practical reset-network implementation flows.
